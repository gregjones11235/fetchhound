FetchHound

From human–canine to human–robot object search: learning a robot's social attention policy from the way a dog reads its human.

Ivy He · Jiajie (Jayson) Feng · Jiayu (Alec) Zhu · Madeline Pelgrim · Daphna Buchsbaum · Stefanie Tellex
Brown University, Providence, RI, USA
Motivation

A home robot should search the way a dog does — by reading the human, not waiting for orders.

Picture a robot looking for an object in a room full of look-alike candidates, where its human partner offers only partial hints about where the target is and what it looks like. Succeeding at this draws on three abilities: perceiving the objects and the partner, moving its body and sensors, and — the part we study — deciding where to direct attention next.

We frame that decision as the agent's social attention policy: at every moment of the search, how should it split its attention between the candidate objects and the person who holds the missing information? Most deployed human–robot systems still wait for explicit instructions, and while interpreting implicit cues like gaze and pointing has been studied, it has never been learned as an end-to-end policy.

The Core Idea

Outside robotics, dogs already solve this. They locate objects with us across no shared language — by alternating attention between the human and the world.

Canine-cognition research describes this coordination as a small set of discrete regimes — following a human's gaze, exploring on their own before seeking help, alternating gaze to establish joint reference, and checking back when stuck. The conditions that shaped these behaviors in dogs closely match what a mobile robot faces: similar sensing channels, and the same underlying task. So we ask a single question:

Can attention-coordination patterns extracted from real human–dog interaction serve as effective priors for a robot's social attention policy?

Preliminary Results

The policy transfers — but high success isn't the same as correct attention.

We compare three temporal aggregators against hand-crafted baselines. Learned policies beat the naive nearest-cup heuristic on task success in both domains, confirming they use the human's referential signal rather than geometry alone. Yet matching the dog's attention pattern stays hard.
